# Evidence Review: Early Literacy, Numeracy and Child ASR 2026-09-28

Policy version: `evidence-policy/1`
Review date: 2026-09-28
Reviewer: Claude (agent), for the ai_edu meta-prompt sections 2.2, 2.3 and 21, and PLAN item "Research early literacy and numeracy for a 5-year-old pre-reader, plus child speech recognition".

Learner context: a young US native English speaker, not yet reading. Goals: letters, letter sounds, short words and onward; basic number skills; possibly a system that listens to the child speak.

Method: For each topic, searched for systematic reviews and meta-analyses first, then RCTs and large field studies. Abstracts were read from primary indexes (OpenAlex, Europe PMC, ERIC API, Semantic Scholar, arXiv). DOIs were checked against Crossref for title, authors, year and venue, except where noted under "Unverified / not found". A number is recorded only if it appeared in a primary abstract or full text that was retrieved. Figures seen only in search snippets, press coverage or blogs are left out and listed as unverified.

Full text read: NELP 2008 report (Tables 2.1, 3.1-3.8), Piasta and Wagner 2010 (PMC), Kim et al. 2021, Price-Mohr and Price 2020 (accepted manuscript), Godwin et al. 2016 (author manuscript), EEF GraphoGame Rime executive summary, and the ASR papers by Ying 2025, Fan 2024, Jain 2023, Janssens 2024, Yeung 2018, Zhu 2022 and Block Medin 2024. Everything else is abstract-level.

Limits: Moderator findings in meta-analyses are between-study comparisons and are confounded. Most literacy and numeracy trials are classroom- or small-group-based with disadvantaged or at-risk samples; a single child at home with a parent is an extrapolation almost everywhere. No risk-of-bias grading was done beyond what sources report. Several ASR sources are preprints or workshop papers.

Tier key (meta-prompt 2.2): A high confidence, B moderate with boundary conditions, C promising/experimental, D unsupported/disfavored. Expert guidance without a systematic evidence base is labeled "expert opinion".

## 1. Phonemic / phonological awareness (PA) training, ages 4-6

- Tier: A for gains in PA and short-term decoding. B for lasting reading gains, and only when PA is taught with letters.
- Population: preschool through grade 1, typical and at-risk.
- Outcomes: PA, word reading, spelling; some follow-up.
- Effect sizes seen:
  - Ehri et al. 2001 (NRP, 52 studies, 96 comparisons): PA d = 0.86, reading d = 0.53, spelling d = 0.59.
  - Bus and van IJzendoorn 1999 (US randomized or matched): PA d = 0.73, reading d = 0.70; long-term reading d = 0.16.
  - NELP 2008 (code-focused interventions, random effects): PA 0.82 (51 studies), reading 0.44 (36), spelling 0.61. Preschool subgroup: PA 0.87, reading 0.75 (4 studies). Reading outcome by training content: PA only 0.19 (not significant, 10 studies), PA plus alphabet knowledge 0.31 (13), PA plus phonics 0.66 (17).
  - Rehfeld et al. 2022 (children suspected of reading disability, 138 manuscripts): composite g = 0.511; segmenting 0.571, first-sound identification 0.428, blending 0.341, deletion 0.248.
  - Suggate 2016 (71 groups, N = 8,161, mean follow-up 11 months): posttest 0.37 falling to 0.22; PA effects held better than phonics or preschool/kindergarten effects.
- Moderators: larger with letters, with 1-2 PA skills rather than many, in small groups, and at 5-18 total hours (Ehri 2001). Rehfeld 2022 found no dose-response and recommends including graphemes.
- Causal support for the mechanism: Hulme et al. 2012 found gains in letter-sound knowledge and phoneme awareness fully mediated word-reading gains five months after an RCT ended. Melby-Lervag et al. 2012 (235 studies) found phoneme awareness the strongest correlate of word reading.
- Uncertainty: low for direction; moderate for dose. The 5-18 hour band is a correlational moderator, not a tested ceiling.
- Differs from prior: 2.3 has no literacy anchors. Proposed anchor: teach phoneme awareness together with letters, focused on segmenting and blending; oral-only PA games are not sufficient for reading.
- Citations:
  - Ehri, Nunes, Willows, Schuster, Yaghoub-Zadeh, Shanahan (2001). Phonemic awareness instruction helps children learn to read. Reading Research Quarterly 36(3):250-287. doi:10.1598/RRQ.36.3.2
  - Bus, van IJzendoorn (1999). Phonological awareness and early reading: a meta-analysis of experimental training studies. Journal of Educational Psychology 91(3):403-414. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.91.3.403
  - National Early Literacy Panel (2008). Developing Early Literacy. National Institute for Literacy. ERIC ED504224 (full text)
  - Rehfeld, Kirkpatrick, O'Guinn, Renbarger (2022). A meta-analysis of phonemic awareness instruction provided to children suspected of having a reading disability. LSHSS 53(4):1177-1201. doi:10.1044/2022_LSHSS-21-00160
  - Suggate (2016). A meta-analysis of the long-term effects of phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, and reading comprehension interventions. Journal of Learning Disabilities 49(1):77-96. doi:10.1177/0022219414528540
  - Hulme, Bowyer-Crane, Carroll, Duff, Snowling (2012). The causal role of phoneme awareness and letter-sound knowledge in learning to read. Psychological Science 23(6):572-577. doi:10.1177/0956797611435921
  - Melby-Lervag, Lyster, Hulme (2012). Phonological skills and their role in learning to read. Psychological Bulletin 138(2):322-352. doi:10.1037/a0026744

## 2. Systematic phonics (synthetic vs other approaches)

- Tier: A for systematic phonics begun in kindergarten or earlier. B for synthetic over analytic phonics specifically.
- Population: K-6 in the NRP; poor readers in Cochrane.
- Effect sizes seen:
  - Ehri, Nunes, Stahl, Willows 2001 (NRP, 66 comparisons from 38 experiments): d = 0.41 overall; kindergarten/grade 1 d = 0.55; after grade 1 d = 0.27. Synthetic and larger-unit phonics gave similar advantages. Small groups and classes were not less effective than tutoring.
  - Suggate 2010 (85 studies, N = 7,522): phonics interventions most effective up to grade 1.
  - McArthur et al. 2018 (Cochrane, 14 studies, 923 poor readers): regular-word accuracy SMD 0.51 [0.13, 0.90] (low quality); fluency 0.45 (moderate quality); comprehension 0.28 [-0.07, 0.62].
  - Stuebing et al. 2008 reanalysis: small-to-moderate effects remain; largest with more comprehensive, intensive instruction.
  - Johnston and Watson 2004 (Clackmannanshire): 5-year-old entrants taught synthetic phonics outperformed analytic-phonics groups on reading, spelling and PA, including when letter-learning speed was equated. Design quality not verified (see Unverified).
- Dissent: Bowers 2020 reviews 12 meta-analyses and argues superiority over alternatives is not established. Torgerson et al. 2019 (tertiary review, abstract only) notes the definitive trial comparing phonics approaches was never run.
- Uncertainty: low that systematic phonics helps early readers; moderate on synthetic vs analytic.
- Differs from prior: no 2.3 anchor. Proposed: systematic, explicit letter-sound instruction with blending as the core of the reading track.
- Citations:
  - Ehri, Nunes, Stahl, Willows (2001). Systematic phonics instruction helps students learn to read. Review of Educational Research 71(3):393-447. doi:10.3102/00346543071003393
  - Suggate (2010). Why what we teach depends on when. Developmental Psychology 46(6):1556-1579. doi:10.1037/a0020612
  - McArthur et al. (2018). Phonics training for English-speaking poor readers. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews CD009115.pub3. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD009115.pub3
  - Stuebing, Barth, Cirino, Francis, Fletcher (2008). A response to recent reanalyses of the National Reading Panel report. Journal of Educational Psychology 100(1):123-134. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.100.1.123
  - Johnston, Watson (2004). Accelerating the development of reading, spelling and phonemic awareness skills in initial readers. Reading and Writing 17(4):327-357. doi:10.1023/B:READ.0000032666.66359.62
  - Bowers (2020). Reconsidering the evidence that systematic phonics is more effective than alternative methods. Educational Psychology Review 32(3):681-705. doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09515-y
  - Torgerson, Brooks, Gascoine, Higgins (2019). Phonics: reading policy and the evidence of effectiveness from a systematic 'tertiary' review. Research Papers in Education 34(2):208-238. doi:10.1080/02671522.2017.1420816 (abstract only)

## 3. Letter-sound knowledge, alphabet instruction and teaching sequence

- Tier: A for alphabet knowledge as a predictor. B for multicomponent alphabet instruction. C for faster pacing. No comparative evidence for any specific letter order (s-a-t-p-i-n or otherwise); treat order choice as an untested design decision, not an evidence-backed one.
- Predictors (NELP 2008, Table 2.1, kindergarten or earlier to later decoding): alphabet knowledge r = 0.50 (52 studies, 7,570 children); writing letters or own name r = 0.49; PA r = 0.40; rapid naming r = 0.40; oral language r = 0.33.
- Instruction (Piasta and Wagner 2010, 63 studies, 8,468 participants): multicomponent instruction on letter-sound knowledge gU = 0.65 (k = 36); on letter-name knowledge 0.43 (k = 27). Alphabet-only instruction on immediate reading 0.21 [0.02, 0.40] (k = 6), at follow-up 0.11 [-0.12, 0.35]. No effect on PA or spelling.
- Sequence and order evidence:
  - Roberts, Vadasy, Sanders 2019 (RCT, 94 preschoolers, 10 weeks, 12-15 min/day): no main effect of letter-names-first vs letter-sounds-first; plain paired-associate practice (letter shape linked to spoken label) did best for native English speakers.
  - Piasta, Purpura, Wagner 2010 (RCT, n = 58, 34 lessons of 15 min): combined name plus sound instruction beat sound-only on letter-sound learning.
  - Sunde, Furnes, Lundetrae 2020 (natural experiment, Norway): introducing letters faster than one per week gave better letter knowledge, word reading and spelling; low performers gained most.
  - Justice et al. 2006 (339 four-year-olds, descriptive): children learn earlier the letters in their own name, letters early in the alphabet, letters whose sound is in the name, and early-developing consonant sounds.
  - Treiman et al. 1998 (660 children, 3.5-7.5 years): sounds are easier when the sound is in the letter's name, especially at the start (b /bi/ easier than l /el/).
  - Vousden 2008 (corpus analysis): grapheme frequencies are Zipfian; teaching the most frequent correspondences first is the rational order. Not a trial.
  - Letters and Sounds (s a t p i n) and Jolly Phonics (s a t i p n) orders: no comparative trial found in ERIC, Crossref or web search. Their stated rationale (early letters form many CVC words) is practical, not tested.
- Uncertainty: low for predictor strength; high for order.
- Citations:
  - Piasta, Wagner (2010). Developing early literacy skills: a meta-analysis of alphabet learning and instruction. Reading Research Quarterly 45(1):8-38. doi:10.1598/RRQ.45.1.2
  - Roberts, Vadasy, Sanders (2019). Preschoolers' alphabet learning: cognitive, teaching sequence, and English proficiency influences. Reading Research Quarterly 54(3):413-437. doi:10.1002/rrq.242
  - Piasta, Purpura, Wagner (2010). Fostering alphabet knowledge development: a comparison of two instructional approaches. Reading and Writing 23(6):607-626. doi:10.1007/s11145-009-9174-x
  - Sunde, Furnes, Lundetrae (2020). Does introducing the letters faster boost the development of children's letter knowledge, word reading and spelling in the first year of school? Scientific Studies of Reading 24(2):141-158. doi:10.1080/10888438.2019.1615491
  - Justice, Pence, Bowles, Wiggins (2006). An investigation of four hypotheses concerning the order by which 4-year-old children learn the alphabet letters. Early Childhood Research Quarterly 21(3):374-389. doi:10.1016/j.ecresq.2006.07.010
  - Treiman, Tincoff, Rodriguez, Mouzaki, Francis (1998). The foundations of literacy: learning the sounds of letters. Child Development 69(6):1524-1540. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.tb06175.x
  - Vousden (2008). Units of English spelling-to-sound mapping. Applied Cognitive Psychology 22(2):247-272. doi:10.1002/acp.1371
  - NELP (2008), as above.

## 4. Decodable text and CVC blending

- Tier: C/D for decodable text as a driver of outcomes. B for blending practice as part of phonics (indirect support).
- Findings: Cheatham and Allor 2012 review: decodability increases decoding-strategy use and immediate accuracy; authors recommend selecting text on multiple criteria. Jenkins et al. 2004 (RCT, at-risk first graders, 25 weeks, 85% vs 11% decodable books with the same phonics program): no difference between text groups on any posttest. Mesmer 2005: highly decodable text raised application of letter-sound knowledge and accuracy after 14 days. Mesmer 2010 (74 first graders): accuracy inconclusive; fluency higher in leveled text. Price-Mohr and Price 2020 (UK, ages 4-5, n = 18 per arm): low-decodable books better for passage comprehension (d = 0.96, p = .029); very small study.
- Blending: the reading advantage of PA plus phonics (0.66) over PA alone (0.19) in NELP, and Piasta and Wagner's weak transfer from alphabet-only instruction, indirectly support explicit blending of known letter sounds into words. No blending-specific trial with verified metadata was found.
- Uncertainty: high for decodable text; moderate for blending.
- Citations:
  - Cheatham, Allor (2012). The influence of decodability in early reading text on reading achievement. Reading and Writing 25(9):2223-2246. doi:10.1007/s11145-011-9355-2
  - Jenkins, Peyton, Sanders, Vadasy (2004). Effects of reading decodable texts in supplemental first-grade tutoring. Scientific Studies of Reading 8(1):53-85. doi:10.1207/s1532799xssr0801_4
  - Mesmer (2005). Text decodability and the first-grade reader. Reading and Writing Quarterly 21(1):61-86. doi:10.1080/10573560590523667
  - Mesmer (2010). Textual scaffolds for developing fluency in beginning readers. Literacy Research and Instruction 49(1):20-39. doi:10.1080/19388070802613450
  - Price-Mohr, Price (2020). A comparison of children aged 4-5 years learning to read through instructional texts containing either a high or a low proportion of phonically-decodable words. Early Childhood Education Journal 48(1):39-47. doi:10.1007/s10643-019-00970-4

## 5. Letter formation, handwriting and letter learning

- Tier: C. Direction favors writing letters by hand; evidence is small or mechanistic.
- Findings: Longcamp et al. 2005 (76 children aged 3-5, 3 weeks): handwriting practice beat typing on later letter recognition, in the older children only. James and Engelhardt 2012 (fMRI, 5-year-olds): letter perception recruited the reading network only after printing, not after typing or tracing. Ray et al. 2022 (systematic review, 17 kindergarten studies, 3,343 participants): letter-writing fluency linked to letter-name and letter-sound knowledge; mostly correlational. Wiley and Rapp 2021 (adults, N = 42): handwriting beat typing and visual practice. NELP: writing letters or own name predicts decoding r = 0.49. Santangelo and Graham 2016 reports handwriting-instruction effects on legibility and writing only, not reading.
- Uncertainty: high; transfer from adults and fMRI to learning outcomes is inferential.
- Citations:
  - Longcamp, Zerbato-Poudou, Velay (2005). The influence of writing practice on letter recognition in preschool children. Acta Psychologica 119(1):67-79. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2004.10.019
  - James, Engelhardt (2012). The effects of handwriting experience on functional brain development in pre-literate children. Trends in Neuroscience and Education 1(1):32-42. doi:10.1016/j.tine.2012.08.001
  - Ray, Dally, Rowlandson, Tam, Lane (2022). The relationship of handwriting ability and literacy in kindergarten: a systematic review. Reading and Writing 35(5):1119-1155. doi:10.1007/s11145-021-10224-8
  - Wiley, Rapp (2021). The effects of handwriting experience on literacy learning. Psychological Science 32(7):1086-1103. doi:10.1177/0956797621993111

## 6. Shared and dialogic reading, print referencing

- Tier: B for oral language and vocabulary, with conditions. B for print referencing on print knowledge (classroom trials; home use is an extrapolation).
- Effect sizes seen:
  - Mol, Bus, de Jong, Smeets 2008 (dialogic reading, 16 studies): expressive vocabulary d = .59 (k = 9); the effect "reduced substantially when children were older (4 to 5 years old) or when they were at risk" (subgroup values not seen).
  - Dowdall et al. 2020 (19 RCTs of parent book-sharing training, N = 2,594, ages 1-6): expressive language d = 0.41, receptive d = 0.26; low dosage gave minimal impact; child age unrelated to outcome.
  - Noble et al. 2019: overall g = 0.194; against active controls g = 0.028 (p = .703); at follow-up g = 0.139 (not significant).
  - Mol, Bus, de Jong 2009 (31 studies, n = 2,049, early education settings): moderate oral-language effect; 7% of variance in kindergartners' alphabet knowledge; experimenter effects did not replicate with teachers.
  - Bus, van IJzendoorn, Pellegrini 1995 (correlational): parent-preschooler reading frequency d = .59 across language and literacy outcomes.
  - Senechal and LeFevre 2002 (5-year longitudinal, N = 168): storybook exposure predicted vocabulary and listening comprehension; parent teaching of letters and words predicted early literacy skills. Two separate paths.
  - Print referencing: Justice et al. 2009 (RCT, 106 preschoolers) gave medium-sized gains in print concepts, alphabet knowledge and name writing; Piasta et al. 2012 (N = 550 four-year-olds) found effects on reading, spelling and comprehension two years later. Numeric sizes not in abstracts.
- Uncertainty: moderate. Effects shrink against active controls and at age 4-5 in one synthesis.
- Implication for the code track: book reading alone will not teach letter-sound skills (Senechal and LeFevre); keep shared reading for vocabulary and language, and teach code skills explicitly.
- Citations:
  - Mol, Bus, de Jong, Smeets (2008). Added value of dialogic parent-child book readings: a meta-analysis. Early Education and Development 19(1):7-26. doi:10.1080/10409280701838603
  - Mol, Bus, de Jong (2009). Interactive book reading in early education. Review of Educational Research 79(2):979-1007. doi:10.3102/0034654309332561
  - Dowdall, Melendez-Torres, Murray, Gardner, Hartford, Cooper (2020). Shared picture book reading interventions for child language development. Child Development 91(2):e383-e399. doi:10.1111/cdev.13225
  - Noble, Sala, Peter, Lingwood, Rowland, Gobet, Pine (2019). The impact of shared book reading on children's language skills: a meta-analysis. Educational Research Review 28:100290. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2019.100290
  - Bus, van IJzendoorn, Pellegrini (1995). Joint book reading makes for success in learning to read. Review of Educational Research 65(1):1-21. doi:10.3102/00346543065001001
  - Senechal, LeFevre (2002). Parental involvement in the development of children's reading skill. Child Development 73(2):445-460. doi:10.1111/1467-8624.00417

## 7. Digital and app-based early literacy; adult co-use

- Tier: B for well-designed apps targeting constrained skills (letters, PA), at small sizes. D for GraphoGame used child-alone. C for high-adult-interaction app use. B for adult mediation as a moderator (convergent, but moderator-level, not direct trials).
- Effect sizes seen:
  - Kim, Gilbert, Yu, Gale 2021 (36 studies, preschool-grade 3, full text): overall +0.31 SD. Constrained skills 0.31 vs unconstrained 0.14; preschool 0.35 vs K-3 0.17; researcher-developed measures 0.43 vs standardized 0.17. Trim-and-fill imputed six missing studies. Mostly school-based.
  - Verhoeven et al. 2020 (59 studies, N = 6,786, preschool/kindergarten): 0.28 [0.21, 0.35]; larger in non-randomized studies and against teaching-as-usual.
  - McTigue et al. 2020 (GraphoGame, 19 studies meta-analyzed): word reading g = -0.02; high-adult-interaction studies averaged g = 0.48.
  - EEF GraphoGame Rime trial (398 low-attaining Year 2 pupils, England): reading ES -0.06 [-0.23, 0.12]; rated "very high security".
  - Takacs, Swart, Bus 2015 (43 studies): technology-enhanced storybooks, comprehension g+ = 0.17, expressive vocabulary 0.20; multimedia (animation, music) helped, interactive hotspots, games and dictionaries distracted.
  - Takacs et al. 2014: multimedia stories beat print read without an adult, but did not differ from print shared with an adult.
  - Furenes, Kucirkova, Bus 2021 (39 studies, ages 1-8): adult mediation of print books was more effective than digital enhancements used independently; story-congruent enhancements beat paper.
  - Savva, Higgins, Beckmann 2022: e-books vs print g = 0.25; digital features plus adult scaffolding did best.
- Uncertainty: moderate. Few home-based, parent-present app RCTs with 5-year-olds; none found.
- Differs from prior: consistent with the GenAI anchor (tool design and augment vs substitute matter). Adds: child-alone phonics games have a null pooled effect in the best-studied case.
- Citations:
  - Kim, Gilbert, Yu, Gale (2021). Measures matter: a meta-analysis of the effects of educational apps on preschool to grade 3 children's literacy and math skills. AERA Open 7. doi:10.1177/23328584211004183
  - Verhoeven, Voeten, van Setten, Segers (2020). Computer-supported early literacy intervention effects in preschool and kindergarten. Educational Research Review 30:100325. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2020.100325
  - McTigue, Solheim, Zimmer, Uppstad (2020). Critically reviewing GraphoGame across the world. Reading Research Quarterly 55(1):45-73. doi:10.1002/rrq.256

## 8. Screen time at age 5

- Tier: expert opinion for AAP 2016 and WHO 2019 guidance. C for the observational associations behind them.
- Findings: AAP 2016 (abstract) says research on ages 0-5 "still remains limited" and emphasizes parent-child shared media use. WHO 2019 covers children under 5, so a 5-year-old is at the edge of its scope. Madigan et al. 2020 (42 studies, N = 18,905, correlational): screen quantity r = -0.14, background TV r = -0.19, educational programs r = +0.13, co-viewing r = +0.16. Madigan et al. 2019 (cohort, N = 2,441): screen time at 36 months predicted lower developmental screening scores at 60 months, beta = -0.08 [-0.13, -0.02]; maternal report, small association.
- Uncertainty: high for causality; direction consistent that co-use and educational content associate with better language than solo or background use.

## 9. Early numeracy: predictors, interventions and fade-out

- Tier: A that early number skill predicts later math (correlational). A for short-term intervention effects; B for lasting ones. A that fade-out occurs.
- Predictors: Duncan et al. 2007 (6 longitudinal datasets): early math skills had the greatest predictive power, then reading, then attention. Jordan et al. 2009: kindergarten number competence predicted level and growth of math through grade 3. Watts et al. 2014: math at 54 months predicts math at age 15.
- Interventions: Nelson and McMaster 2019 (34 studies): g = 0.64 [0.52, 0.76]; larger when including counting with 1-to-1 correspondence and at 8 weeks or less. Wang et al. 2016 (29 studies): "moderate to large" (no number in abstract); larger with a single content strand, 120-150 min/week, individual delivery and researcher-made tests. Clements and Sarama 2008 (Building Blocks, 36 classrooms): ES 1.07 vs control; Clements et al. 2011 (1,375 preschoolers): g = 0.72. Benavides-Varela et al. 2020 (digital interventions, children with math difficulties, 15 RCTs): 0.55; games added nothing over drill or tutoring software.
- Fade-out: Bailey et al. 2016 attributed about 72% of Building Blocks fade-out to preexisting treatment-control differences. Bailey et al. 2020 (PSPI): fade-out is widespread and not merely a measurement artifact; persistence depends on skills targeted and on later complementary environments.
- Finger counting: Tier C. Poletti et al. 2025 (N = 328 French kindergartners who did not already finger-count): training raised addition accuracy from 37.3% to 77.1% vs 39.6% to 47.8% for passive control. Schild et al. 2020: 10 weeks of finger training did not beat an active control.

## 10. Linear number board games; symbolic vs approximate-number training

- Tier: B for linear number board games (small pooled effect). A for symbolic magnitude and number-line skill as correlates of math. D for non-symbolic approximate number system (ANS) training as a route to symbolic math.
- Board games: Siegler and Ramani 2008: four 15-minute sessions of a linear number game eliminated a low- vs middle-income gap in numerical estimation; color-board control had no effect. Ramani and Siegler 2008 (mean age 5.4, about 1 hour total): gains in magnitude comparison, number-line estimation, counting and numeral identification, retained 9 weeks later. Siegler and Ramani 2009: linear board beat circular board. Scalise, Daubert, Ramani 2018 (mean age 4.7, four 15-minute sessions): card game improved symbolic comparison. Nelson et al. 2025 meta-analysis (18 studies, 123 effect sizes): g = .21 [.08, .34]; dosage, co-player and board characteristics moderated. Balladares et al. 2023 systematic review: intervention-vs-control differences significant in only 32% of tasks.
- Magnitude: Schneider et al. 2017 (N = 17,201): symbolic comparison r = .302 vs non-symbolic r = .241 with math. Schneider et al. 2018: number-line estimation r = .443 (ages 4-14). Qiu et al. 2021: ANS training effect on symbolic math g = .11 [-.01, .22], and -.04 after small-study correction. Szkudlarek et al. 2021 failed to replicate adult ANS training benefits (N = 318).

## 11. Session length, attention and spacing for 4-6 year olds

- Tier: no evidence establishes an optimal session length for 5-year-olds. C for shorter activity blocks (observational). B for spacing sessions across days (small consistent lab studies). C for sparse visual environments.
- Findings: Godwin et al. 2016 (full text, K-4 classrooms): "there is a dearth of evidence-based guidelines" on activity duration; on-task odds were 1.42 times higher for a 10-minute than a 30-minute activity (observational, confounded); whole-group formats had the lowest on-task rates. Fisher, Godwin, Seltman 2014: kindergartners in a heavily decorated room were more distracted and learned less than in a sparse room. Vlach and Sandhofer 2012 (ages 5-7, N = 36): spaced science lessons produced better generalization than massed. Vlach, Sandhofer, Kornell 2008 (3-year-olds) and Childers and Tomasello 2002 (2-year-olds; number of different days of exposure mattered most) point the same way. Effective board-game doses were four 15-minute sessions (section 10); Roberts et al. 2019 used 12-15 min/day.
- Differs from prior: consistent with meta-prompt guidance to learn the individual session envelope and default short for young children. Adds tier-B support for spreading exposure across days.

## 13. Reading tutors that listen

## 14. Local / offline ASR and privacy

- Tier: D for any local engine on this task until measured on the learner.
- Options (factual, not endorsements): whisper.cpp and faster-whisper (MIT) run Whisper weights offline; the Whisper child results above apply to the same weights, and effects of quantization on child speech are untested. NVIDIA Parakeet (CC-BY-4.0) and Canary (canary-1b CC-BY-NC-4.0, canary-1b-flash CC-BY-4.0) are open-weight; zero-shot OGI results are in section 12. Vosk/Kaldi (Apache-2.0): no child benchmark found. Moonshine: no child benchmark; license must be checked by hand.
- COPPA (FTC final rule, 90 FR 16918, April 22, 2025; compliance mostly due April 22, 2026): personal information includes audio files containing a child's voice and voiceprints. An exception allows collecting a child's voice audio without parental consent only to respond to the child's specific request, used for no other purpose, and deleted immediately after. Whether audio processed only on-device counts as "collected online" was not resolved here; it is a legal question.
- Relation to prior: meta-prompt section 21 already requires minimizing child data. On-device ASR with no audio retention fits it; the COPPA analysis matters only if the system leaves the household.

## Differences from meta-prompt priors

1. Section 2.3 has no early literacy or numeracy anchors. Proposed tier-A/B anchors: phoneme awareness taught with letters (segment and blend); systematic, explicit letter-sound instruction; early symbolic number skill (counting, numerals, number line).
2. The GenAI anchor extends to child apps: GraphoGame used child-alone has a null pooled effect (g = -0.02), while adult-interaction studies averaged g = 0.48. Adult mediation is the most consistent moderator across the app and e-book literature.
3. The session guidance in the meta-prompt holds. No study establishes an optimal session length for 5-year-olds; the evidence favors short blocks and several sessions across days.
4. App and intervention effect sizes shrink sharply on standardized measures (Kim 2021: 0.43 vs 0.17). Mastery checks should use items the app did not train on directly.
5. Early math gains fade (Bailey 2020). Short-term gains are not evidence of durable learning.

## Design implications for a 5-year-old pre-reader

### Findings (directly supported above, with tier)

- F1 (A): Phoneme awareness instruction works best combined with letters; PA alone gave a non-significant reading effect in NELP (0.19) vs 0.66 for PA plus phonics.
- F2 (A): Systematic phonics has its largest effects at kindergarten and grade 1 (d = 0.55).
- F3 (A): Alphabet knowledge is the strongest single predictor of later decoding (r = 0.50); writing letters or own name is close (r = 0.49).
- F4 (B/C): Combined letter name plus sound instruction beat sound-only in one RCT; names-first vs sounds-first made no difference in another; faster introduction (more than one letter per week) did better in a natural experiment.
- F5: No specific letter order has comparative evidence.
- F6 (B): Shared reading helps oral language, but book reading alone did not predict early code skills; parent teaching of letters and words did.
- F7 (B/D): Literacy apps give small effects that shrink on standardized measures; child-alone GraphoGame had a null pooled effect; adult involvement is the most consistent positive moderator; interactive hotspots and games in e-books distract.
- F8 (A/B): Early number competence predicts later math; linear numbered board games give a small pooled benefit (g = .21); symbolic number work is better supported than dot-acuity training (ANS training g near zero after bias correction).
- F9 (B/C): Spacing across days helps young children; shorter activities associate with more on-task behavior; sparse visual environments helped kindergartners attend.
- F10 (D): Off-the-shelf ASR on ages 4-7 had zero-shot WERs of about 39-44% (Whisper); single spoken numbers from 5-year-olds were recognized 48% of the time by Whisper. Fine-tuning on in-domain child speech brings WER below 10%, and prompting helped (48% to 88% on numbers).

### Inferences (not directly tested for this learner; label as hypotheses and instrument)

- I1: A letter-sound track should pair each sound with its written letter from the start and move quickly to blending known sounds into CVC words. Choose an order that makes words early and weights frequent graphemes and own-name letters; record the order as a policy parameter since no order has evidence.
- I2: Include printing letters by hand (paper or stylus) alongside recognition tasks. In the one fMRI study, printing recruited the reading network and tracing or typing did not.
- I3: Design every session for a parent in the loop. Child-alone use has the weakest evidence; the parent can serve as the correctness judge for spoken answers.
- I4: Keep screens sparse; avoid decorative animations and tap-for-reward hotspots that are not tied to the learning target.
- I5: Math track: counting with 1-to-1 correspondence, numeral recognition, and a linear numbered track game where the child names the numbers moved through; symbolic comparison with dots-to-numeral links. Skip standalone dot-acuity drills.
- I6: Sessions of roughly 10-15 minutes several days per week fit all the dose data seen, but nothing shows this is optimal. Learn the envelope per meta-prompt guidance.
- I7: Measure mastery with delayed, untrained items (new CVC words, unseen numerals) to avoid the researcher-measure inflation seen in app meta-analyses.
- I8: ASR should not be the sole judge of a 5-year-old's letter sounds or words. If used, run it on-device, score against a closed set (target plus a few distractors) rather than open transcription, keep no audio, fall back to parent confirmation on low confidence, and measure accuracy on this child's own recordings before trusting it. Isolated phonemes are the least-studied and likely hardest case.
