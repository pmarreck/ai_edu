# Evidence Review 2026-09-28

Policy version: `evidence-policy/1`
Review date: 2026-09-28
Reviewer: Claude (agent), for the ai_edu meta-prompt sections 1.2, 2.3, 14.1 and 15.

Method: For each technique, searched for systematic reviews and meta-analyses first, then preregistered RCTs and large field experiments. Abstracts were read from primary indexes (Europe PMC, OpenAlex, ERIC API, arXiv, PMC full text for Kestin et al.). A number is recorded only if I saw it in the primary abstract or full text. Where I only saw a figure in a search-engine snippet or a secondary blog, it is left out and listed under "Unverified / not found".

Limits: Abstract-level review for most sources; full text read only for Kestin et al. 2025. Many publisher pages (PNAS, Nature, ScienceDirect, Springer) blocked automated fetches, so moderator details inside papers were not checked. No risk-of-bias grading was done beyond what the sources report. Most evidence comes from adolescents, undergraduates and adults; direct evidence for young children (under about 7) is thin for nearly every technique, and a tier here does not transfer automatically to that age band. Several 2025-2026 GenAI items are preprints or working papers.

Tier key (meta-prompt 2.2): A high confidence, B moderate with boundary conditions, C promising/experimental, D unsupported/disfavored.

## 1. Spaced / distributed practice

- Tier: A (retention of studied material). B for mathematics specifically.
- Population: lab participants (mostly adults) in the core synthesis; school and university students in classroom meta-analyses.
- Domains: verbal recall (core); mathematics; mixed classroom content.
- Outcomes: delayed retention. Transfer less studied.
- Moderators: optimal inter-study gap grows with the retention interval (Cepeda 2006). Effect smaller in mathematics and smaller when material is embedded in a real course (Murray 2025: overall g = 0.28; isolated material g = 0.43; course-embedded g = 0.24). Spacing retrieval episodes beats massing them (Latimier 2021: g = 0.74), but expanding schedules were not better than uniform ones (g = 0.034); expanding helped more as the number of tests rose.
- Uncertainty: low for direction, moderate for magnitude outside word lists.
- Differs from prior: No on direction. Refinement: do not hard-code expanding intervals as superior; expect smaller gains in math.
- Citations:
  - Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, Rohrer (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks. Psychological Bulletin 132(3):354-380. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
  - Latimier, Peyre, Ramus (2021). A meta-analytic review of the benefit of spacing out retrieval practice episodes on retention. Educational Psychology Review. doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8
  - Murray, Horner, Goebel (2025). A meta-analytic review of the effectiveness of spacing and retrieval practice for mathematics learning. Educational Psychology Review. doi:10.1007/s10648-025-10035-1 (abstract via ERIC EJ1478558)
  - Dunlosky et al. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1). doi:10.1177/1529100612453266 (rated distributed practice "high utility")

## 2. Retrieval practice (with / without feedback)

- Tier: A for retention of studied facts and concepts in classrooms. B in mathematics. Feedback: B that it adds to the effect (moderator listed by Yang 2021; size not checked).
- Population: 48,478 students across 222 classroom studies (Yang 2021); broad lab literature.
- Domains: broad; weaker evidence in mathematics.
- Outcomes: retention; some transfer.
- Moderators (Yang 2021 abstract): control-condition strategy, test-format consistency, material matching, corrective feedback, number of test repetitions, timing, duration, design. Recall-type initial tests beat recognition tests (Rowland 2014). In mathematics, testing vs restudy g = 0.18 with a 95% CI crossing zero (Murray 2025).
- Effect sizes seen: classroom overall g = 0.499 (Yang 2021).
- Uncertainty: low for verbal/conceptual material; high for procedural math.
- Differs from prior: Largely holds. Add a domain caveat: in math, retrieval alone is not established; pair it with spacing and worked examples.
- Citations:
  - Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu, Shanks (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning. Psychological Bulletin 147(4):399-435. doi:10.1037/bul0000309
  - Rowland (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention. Psychological Bulletin 140(6):1432-1463. doi:10.1037/a0037559
  - Adesope, Trevisan, Sundararajan (2017). Rethinking the use of tests. Review of Educational Research 87(3):659-701. doi:10.3102/0034654316689306 (abstract confirms benefit over restudy and all comparison conditions; numbers not seen)
  - Murray et al. (2025), as above.

## 3. Interleaving

- Tier: B. Conditional on material type.
- Population: mixed lab and classroom; one large preregistered cluster RCT in grade 7.
- Domains: visual categories (strongest), mathematics (small to large), expository text (ambiguous), word lists (blocking better).
- Outcomes: category induction; delayed math test.
- Moderators: stronger when categories are similar to each other and items within a category vary; stronger for complex material (Brunmair 2019). Overall g = 0.42; paintings g = 0.67; math g = 0.34; words g = -0.39.
- Key trial: 54 grade-7 math classes, 4 months, preregistered; unannounced test one month later, interleaved 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83 (Rohrer 2020).
- Uncertainty: moderate. The Rohrer trial is one study; the meta-analysis shows wide material dependence.
- Differs from prior: Section 2.3 has no interleaving anchor. Add one: use interleaving for discriminating confusable problem types or categories, not for word lists or first exposure to expository text.
- Citations:
  - Brunmair, Richter (2019). Similarity matters: a meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. Psychological Bulletin. doi:10.1037/bul0000209
  - Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig, Cheung (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. Journal of Educational Psychology 112(1):40-52. ERIC EJ1237752

## 4. Worked examples and fading (expertise reversal)

- Tier: A for worked examples during initial acquisition by novices in well-structured domains (math). B for fading as competence rises (supported through expertise reversal; I did not locate a fading-specific meta-analysis).
- Population: elementary through postsecondary (Barbieri 2023); 60 experiments, 5,924 participants (Tetzlaff 2025).
- Domains: mathematics primarily; expertise reversal across domains, less pronounced in humanities and language learning per a secondary summary.
- Outcomes: math performance; learning outcomes by prior knowledge.
- Moderators: Barbieri 2023, g = 0.48 overall; correct examples alone beat incorrect or mixed examples; adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples produced a negative effect relative to examples without prompts. Tetzlaff 2025: low-prior-knowledge learners gain from high support; high-prior-knowledge learners do better with low support; giving help to novices matters more than withholding it from experts.
- Uncertainty: low for novices in math; moderate for how and when to fade.
- Differs from prior: Holds. New constraint: do not stack self-explanation prompts on worked examples by default in math.
- Citations:
  - Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste, Chawla (2023). A meta-analysis of the worked examples effect on mathematics performance. Educational Psychology Review 35:11. doi:10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1 (abstract via ERIC EJ1364058)
  - Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters, Brod (2025). A cornerstone of adaptivity: a meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect. Learning and Instruction 98:102142. doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142 (metadata verified; abstract numbers not retrieved, see Unverified)

## 5. Formative assessment (K-12)

- Tier: B. Small positive average effect.
- Population: K-12.
- Domains: ELA, math, science, reading.
- Outcomes: academic achievement.
- Effect sizes seen: Kingston and Nash 2011, weighted mean 0.20 from 42 effects in 13 usable studies (ELA 0.32, math 0.17, science 0.09); they reject the often-cited 0.40-0.70. Yao et al. 2024, g = 0.25 overall from 258 effects in 118 studies, 0.22 for US studies. Xuan et al. 2022 (reading), ES = 0.19 from 48 studies, 116,051 students.
- Moderators: larger in small studies (250 or fewer students), in quasi-experiments, in high school, and outside North America and Western Europe (Yao 2024; Xuan 2022). Similar across self vs peer assessment and across subjects in Yao 2024. Cultural context mattered in Xuan 2022.
- Uncertainty: moderate. Larger effects in weaker designs suggest the true effect is near the low end.
- Differs from prior: No. "Modest positive average" is accurate.
- Citations:
  - Kingston, Nash (2011). Formative assessment: a meta-analysis and a call for research. Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice 30(4). doi:10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00220.x
  - Yao, Amos, Snider, Brown (2024). The impact of formative assessment on K-12 learning: a meta-analysis. Educational Research and Evaluation 29(7-8). doi:10.1080/13803611.2024.2363831
  - Xuan, Cheung, Sun (2022). The effectiveness of formative assessment for enhancing reading achievement in K-12 classrooms. Frontiers in Psychology. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.990196

## 6. Self-explanation

- Tier: B.
- Population: mixed ages; mostly secondary and postsecondary.
- Domains: problem solving, studying worked problems, studying text.
- Outcomes: learning outcomes, mostly immediate.
- Effect sizes seen: g = 0.55 from 69 effects (Bisra 2018).
- Moderators: Bisra coded 20 moderators (task type, subject, level, inducement type, duration); specific moderator results not read. Barbieri 2023 found self-explanation prompts combined with worked examples hurt math performance relative to examples alone. Dunlosky 2013 rated it moderate utility with limited classroom evidence.
- Uncertainty: moderate; possible dependence on prompt quality and on added time.
- Differs from prior: Consistent with the meta-prompt's "when evidence and context favor it". Add the worked-example interaction as an explicit boundary.
- Citations:
  - Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi, Winne (2018). Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review. doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x (abstract via ERIC EJ1186664)
  - Barbieri et al. (2023), as above.
  - Dunlosky et al. (2013), as above.

## 7. Prediction before observation; generation; problem solving before instruction

- Tier: C for prediction-before-feedback as a distinct technique. B for problem-solving-before-instruction (PS-I) in older learners; C to D for PS-I in grades 2-5 (evidence favors instruction first there).
- Population: prediction work is mostly lab studies with adults and school-age children; PS-I meta-analysis spans grade 2 through university.
- Domains: factual/numerical facts (prediction); math and science concepts (PS-I).
- Outcomes: memory for outcomes; conceptual learning and transfer.
- Findings: Brod 2021 (theoretical review) argues prediction helps mainly through surprise when the prediction is wrong. Brod et al. 2022 (two experiments) found explicit prediction boosted memory for highly unexpected outcomes compared with post hoc judgement. Sinha and Kapur 2021: 53 studies, PS-I over I-PS g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]; 0.37-0.58 with high-fidelity Productive Failure; bias-adjusted estimate g = 0.87. Effects favored I-PS for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills.
- Generation effect: meta-analyses exist (Bertsch 2007; McCurdy 2020, 310 experiments) showing memory benefits for self-generated items, moderated by generation constraint. These are mostly word-list memory studies.
- Uncertainty: high for prediction in real curricula; moderate for PS-I with an age reversal that matters for young learners.
- Differs from prior: The meta-prompt lists prediction-first as a technique and an A/B arm. The evidence does not support prediction-first as a default for young children. Keep it as an instrumented experiment arm and add an age-conditional prior favoring explanation-first below about grade 6.
- Citations:
  - Brod (2021). Predicting as a learning strategy. Psychonomic Bulletin and Review. doi:10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1
  - Brod, Greve, Jolles, Theobald, Galeano-Keiner (2022). Explicitly predicting outcomes enhances learning of expectancy-violating information. Psychonomic Bulletin and Review. doi:10.3758/s13423-022-02124-x
  - Sinha, Kapur (2021). When problem solving followed by instruction works: evidence for Productive Failure. Review of Educational Research 91(5):761-798. doi:10.3102/00346543211019105
  - Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott, McDaniel (2007). The generation effect: a meta-analytic review. Memory and Cognition. doi:10.3758/bf03193441 (abstract not retrieved)
  - McCurdy, Viechtbauer, Sklenar, Frankenstein, Leshikar (2020). Theories of the generation effect and the impact of generation constraint. Psychonomic Bulletin and Review. doi:10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3

## 8. Active breaks and executive function in children

- Tier: C for executive-function benefit. No evidence supports a universal interval.
- Population: ages about 6-16 (school children and adolescents).
- Domains: classroom breaks with physical activity.
- Outcomes: attention, inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility, math.
- Findings: Li et al. 2026 (25 RCTs): pooled estimates favored active breaks for attention (SMD reported as -0.53) and inhibition (-0.49), with considerable heterogeneity; 5-15 minute, single-session formats looked larger in exploratory subgroups that the authors say do not establish a prescription. Melguizo-Ibanez et al. 2026 (35 studies, 7,701 students, RVE): only working memory reached significance, g = 0.14; other cognitive and academic outcomes were not significant. de Andrade et al. 2025 (41 studies, 7,316 participants): small effects favoring cognitively engaging activity; acute effect vs rest only on working-memory accuracy. Reyes-Amigo et al. 2025 systematic review (22 studies): classroom behavior benefits more consistent than EF benefits, EF effects "unclear".
- Moderators: cognitive engagement of the activity; intensity (moderate-to-vigorous associated with larger WM effect, exploratory); duration (exploratory).
- Uncertainty: high. Two 2026 meta-analyses disagree on magnitude.
- Differs from prior: Consistent with "some EF outcomes, no magic interval". Slightly weaker than the prior implies: the most conservative synthesis finds only a small working-memory effect. Justify breaks mainly on learner well-being, requested breaks and behavior, not on EF gains.
- Citations:
  - Li, Shangguan, Zhong, Zhou, Wang, Li (2026). Effects of active breaks on attention and executive function in children and adolescents: a meta-analysis. European Journal of Pediatrics. doi:10.1007/s00431-026-07341-3
  - Melguizo-Ibanez, Zurita-Ortega, Tadeu, Ubago-Jimenez, Alonso Vargas (2026). Classroom active breaks as a physical-educational intervention for executive functions and mathematical outcomes. Educational Psychology Review. doi:10.1007/s10648-026-10146-3
  - de Andrade, Pournara, Pontifex, Venetsanou, Pesce, Vazou (2025). A meta-analysis of physical activity interventions targeting executive functions in children. International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology. doi:10.1080/1612197X.2025.2510251
  - Reyes-Amigo et al. (2025). Effectiveness of school-based active breaks on classroom behavior, executive functions and physical fitness. Frontiers in Public Health. doi:10.3389/fpubh.2025.1469998

## 9. Simulation-based learning

- Tier: B for higher education complex skills. C for K-12 and young children (not reviewed here).
- Population: higher education (145 studies).
- Domains: medical, teacher education, management and others.
- Outcomes: complex skills.
- Effect sizes seen: g = 0.85, SE 0.08, CI [0.69, 1.02] (Chernikova 2020).
- Moderators: scaffolding and technology use both positive. Low prior knowledge learners benefited from examples; high prior knowledge learners from reflection phases.
- Uncertainty: moderate; comparison conditions vary widely.
- Differs from prior: Holds, with a population caveat. The prior's "scaffolding adapted to progression" matches the moderator finding.
- Citations:
  - Chernikova, Heitzmann, Stadler, Holzberger, Seidel, Fischer (2020). Simulation-based learning in higher education: a meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research 90(4):499-541. doi:10.3102/0034654320933544

## 10. Learning-styles matching (meshing hypothesis)

- Tier: D.
- Population: children and adults.
- Domains: general.
- Outcomes: learning outcomes under matched vs unmatched modality.
- Findings: Pashler et al. 2008 found almost no studies with the required crossover design, and several that did contradicted meshing. Clinton-Lisell and Litzinger 2024 (21 studies, 1,712 participants): matched instruction g = 0.31, CI [0.05, 0.57], but only 26% of outcome measures showed the crossover interaction that meshing requires; low study quality; authors judge benefits too small and infrequent for adoption.
- Uncertainty: low for the practical conclusion.
- Differs from prior: No. Note the 2024 pooled g is not evidence for meshing because the crossover was rare.
- Citations:
  - Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, Bjork (2008). Learning styles: concepts and evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest 9(3). doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x
  - Clinton-Lisell, Litzinger (2024). Is it really a neuromyth? A meta-analysis of the learning styles matching hypothesis. Frontiers in Psychology. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732

## 11. GenAI tutors and LLM assistance

Split into two entries because the evidence splits by design.

### 11a. Unguarded, answer-giving LLM access during practice

- Tier: D as a practice aid when unassisted skill is the goal.
- Population: Turkish high-school math students (nearly 1,000); undergraduates in coding.
- Outcomes: unassisted exam after access removed.
- Findings: Bastani et al. 2025 field experiment: GPT-4 access raised practice grades (48% for GPT Base, 127% for the guarded GPT Tutor), but after access was removed GPT Base students scored 17% lower than controls; the guarded tutor largely removed the harm. Lehmann et al. (preprint): two preregistered lab experiments found no effect of LLM access on overall learning; exploratory analyses (not randomized on usage) linked substitution use to less understanding and explanation-seeking use to more, and a wider gap between low and high prior-knowledge students.
- Uncertainty: moderate. One large field experiment plus preregistered null lab results.
- Citations:
  - Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakci, Mariman (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: evidence from high school mathematics. PNAS 122(26). doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122. A correction exists: doi:10.1073/pnas.2518204122 (content not read).
  - Lehmann, Cornelius, Sting (2024, preprint). AI meets the classroom: when do large language models harm learning? arXiv:2409.09047

### 11b. Pedagogically constrained AI tutors or AI-assisted human tutors

- Tier: C. Several positive RCTs, all short, design-specific, and most not yet replicated.
- Findings:
  - Kestin et al. 2025 (Harvard physics, N = 194, two-week crossover): AI tutor at home beat in-class active learning on immediate post-tests; regression effect 0.63, quantile-regression 0.73-1.3 SD; median AI time 49 min vs 60 min in class. Post-tests were taken right after each lesson; no delayed retention test. Authors note the tutor could not reliably scaffold multi-part problems from a system prompt alone.
  - De Simone et al. 2025 (World Bank, Nigeria, first-year senior secondary, 6 weeks, Copilot/GPT-4 for English): 0.31 SD on a combined assessment, 0.23 SD on English; largest effects for girls and higher baseline performers. Working paper.
  - Wang et al. 2024 Tutor CoPilot (900 tutors, 1,800 K-12 students, preregistered analysis): AI guidance to human tutors raised topic mastery by 4 percentage points, 9 points for students of lower-rated tutors. The AI supports the tutor; students do not interact with it directly.
  - LearnLM Team and Eedi 2025 (exploratory RCT, N = 165, five UK secondary schools, human-supervised AI drafts): LearnLM students did at least as well as human-tutored students; 66.2% vs 60.7% on novel problems in subsequent topics. Company technical report.
  - Contractor and Reyes 2026 (preprint, proctored undergraduate RCT): AI access raised immediate unaided test scores 0.27 SD with gains persisting one week; delayed essay gains larger for "augmentation" users; automation users' gains vanished once AI was removed (usage type not randomized).
- Moderators across studies: whether AI withholds answers or supplies them; unaided vs aided outcome; immediate vs delayed test; prior knowledge; novice AI users.
- Uncertainty: high. No meta-analysis I found meets the policy bar. A widely cited ChatGPT meta-analysis (Wang and Fan 2025, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, doi:10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y) was retracted on 2026-04-22 (retraction note doi:10.1057/s41599-026-07310-z).
- Differs from prior: Consistent with "GenAI is not reliably beneficial; augment vs substitute matters". Sharpened: outcomes must be measured unaided and delayed; answer-withholding guardrails are the one design factor with direct causal support (Bastani).
- Citations:
  - Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne, Ponti (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning. Scientific Reports 15:17458. doi:10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
  - De Simone, Tiberti, Barron Rodriguez, Manolio, Mosuro, Dikoru (2025). From chalkboards to chatbots. World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11125. ERIC ED676624
  - Wang, Ribeiro, Robinson, Loeb, Demszky (2024). Tutor CoPilot. EdWorkingPaper 24-1054. ERIC ED661562; arXiv:2410.03017
  - LearnLM Team, Google and Eedi (2025-11-11). AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students: an exploratory RCT in UK classrooms. https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/LearnLM/learnLM_nov25.pdf
  - Contractor, Reyes (2026, preprint). Experimental evidence on the learning impact of generative AI. arXiv:2607.08849

## 12. Mastery learning, intelligent tutoring, Bayesian Knowledge Tracing

- Tier: B for mastery learning programs and for step-based intelligent tutoring systems. BKT itself is a measurement model, not a pedagogy; tier C as this system's mastery estimator until validated against delayed unaided outcomes.
- Findings: Kulik, Kulik, Bangert-Drowns 1990 (108 evaluations): mastery programs raised exam performance, more for weaker students; may increase time on task; self-paced mastery reduced college completion rates. Kulik and Fletcher 2016 (50 evaluations): median ITS effect 0.66 SD, much larger on locally developed tests than standardized ones; small effects with nonconventional controls or flawed implementations. Leite et al. 2025 (preprint, 18 US K-12 studies): g = 0.271; weaker in rural settings; worked examples and immediate measurement among key moderators. Corbett and Anderson 1995 introduced knowledge tracing in the ACT Programming Tutor.
- Uncertainty: moderate; older syntheses; alignment of test to instruction inflates effects.
- Differs from prior: No 2.3 anchor exists. Suggest one: mastery gating helps, but measure mastery on unaided, delayed, standardized-style items to avoid the alignment inflation Kulik and Fletcher describe.
- Citations:
  - Kulik, Kulik, Bangert-Drowns (1990). Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: a meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research 60(2). doi:10.3102/00346543060002265
  - Kulik, Fletcher (2016). Effectiveness of intelligent tutoring systems: a meta-analytic review. Review of Educational Research. doi:10.3102/0034654315581420 (abstract via ERIC EJ1090502)
  - Leite, Zhang, Rana, Hao, Hatch, Kong, Kuang (2025, preprint). Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students? arXiv:2511.04997
  - Corbett, Anderson (1995). Knowledge tracing: modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge. User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction 4(4):253-278. doi:10.1007/BF01099821

## Differences from meta-prompt priors

1. Interleaving and mastery/ITS have no 2.3 anchor. Proposed: interleaving at tier B for confusable categories and problem types; mastery gating at tier B with unaided, delayed mastery checks.
2. Retrieval practice holds for facts and concepts, but in mathematics the testing effect alone is not established (Murray 2025, g = 0.18, CI crosses zero). Spacing still helps in math, at a smaller size (g = 0.28).
3. Expanding spacing schedules are not generally better than uniform ones (Latimier 2021). Do not encode expanding intervals as a fixed rule; treat schedule shape as an experiment arm.
4. Self-explanation prompts layered on worked examples hurt math performance in Barbieri 2023. The two should not be combined by default.
5. Prediction-first and problem-solving-first carry an age reversal: for grades 2-5, instruction-first did better (Sinha and Kapur 2021). For a young learner the default prior should favor explanation-first, with prediction-first kept as an instrumented arm.
6. Active breaks: the newest syntheses disagree (moderate pooled effects in Li 2026 vs only a small working-memory effect in Melguizo-Ibanez 2026). The prior's wording is fine but the benefit claim should be weaker than "can benefit"; justify breaks mainly on well-being and learner request.
7. Simulation evidence at tier B comes from higher education. For children it is an extrapolation (tier C) and needs local measurement.
8. GenAI splits in two: unguarded answer-giving access during practice is tier D for unassisted skill; guarded or tutor-mediated designs are tier C with positive short-horizon RCTs. The strongest pro-AI RCT (Kestin 2025) measured only immediate post-tests.
9. Learning styles remain D. The 2024 meta-analysis's pooled g = 0.31 does not rescue meshing because crossover interactions were rare.
10. Formative assessment holds; current best estimates are about 0.20-0.25 SD, with larger numbers coming from weaker designs.

## Unverified / not found

- Bastani et al. PNAS correction (doi:10.1073/pnas.2518204122): exists; content not read. Standard-deviation effect sizes (for example "-0.19 SD") seen only in a search snippet, not recorded.
- De Simone et al. (Nigeria): sample size, randomization unit, preregistration status and attendance/dose details not confirmed from the paper.
- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 expertise reversal: effect size (d around 0.5) seen only in a search snippet; ScienceDirect, ResearchGate and pedocs fetches failed. Qualitative findings came from a secondary summary.
- Adesope et al. 2017: pooled effect sizes and the claim that feedback was only a minor moderator came from a secondary blog and snippets, not the paper.
- Rowland 2014: feedback and retention-interval moderator results seen only in a snippet; the abstract does not state them.
- Bertsch et al. 2007 generation effect: abstract not retrieved; no effect size recorded.
- Rittle-Johnson, Loehr, Durkin (2017). Promoting self-explanation to improve mathematics learning. ZDM. doi:10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z: located, not read.
- A fading-specific meta-analysis (worked examples with faded steps) was not located; fading tier rests on expertise-reversal evidence.
- ChatGPT meta-analyses reporting g of about 0.57 (Liu 2025, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning; IRRODL meta-analysis): located in search results only; not read and not used. Treat with caution given the Wang and Fan retraction.
- Spaced repetition in medical education (Maye et al. 2026, The Clinical Teacher): seen in search results only.
- Carpenter, Pan, Butler (2022). The science of effective learning with spacing and retrieval practice. Nature Reviews Psychology. doi:10.1038/s44159-022-00089-1: located, not read.
- No meta-analysis was found on prediction-before-observation as a classroom technique, or on simulation-based learning for children under about 10.
